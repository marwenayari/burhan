# Jihad and the Spread of Islam

## BH-JH-001 - Was Islam spread by the sword?

### Question variants
- Islam was imposed by force.
- Muslims spread their religion by the sword.
- Islam was spread by the sword.

### How the objection is framed
The objection merges military expansion with forcing individuals to convert.

### Short answer
Draz addresses the claim directly, separating the existence of wars from the claim that their aim was to force people to believe. He argues from the principle of no compulsion and from the continued presence of non-Muslim religious communities within Muslim societies.

### Detailed answer
These are two different questions. Did wars take place? Yes. Was the rule that the defeated had to change their religion by force? The source argues that it was not, and places the fighting in a political and military context.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- War is not the same as coercion in belief.
- The continued presence of religious minorities contradicts the simple explanation of a comprehensive forced Islam.

### Spoken answer
"Wars in Islamic history are a fact, but that is not the same as saying people were forced to change their religion. Draz distinguishes between political and military fighting and faith itself, and the fact that Jews, Christians and others continued for centuries within Muslim societies makes coercion alone an insufficient explanation of the spread."

### Sources
- Madkhal ila al-Quran al-Karim (Introduction to the Noble Quran), chapter "How the Noble Quran spread in the world", PDF pp. 52–67 approx.

## BH-JH-002 - Do the verses of fighting command killing non-Muslims simply for their disbelief?

### Question variants
- The Quran commands killing the disbelievers wherever they are found.
- Is every non-Muslim a legitimate target?
- How should we understand the verses of fighting?

### How the objection is framed
The objection reads texts about war in isolation from who is addressed, from treaties, from aggression and from context.

### Short answer
Draz presents the verses of fighting within a context of conflict and war, with limits relating to aggression, treaties and ceasing hostilities. A phrase from a specific war context is not turned into a general ruling on every non-Muslim.

### Detailed answer
To understand a verse about fighting, one must identify who is addressed, the event, and the rulings on treaties, peace and aggression that come before and after it.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Context is part of the meaning.
- The rulings of war are not the rulings for everyday dealings with everyone of another religion.

### Spoken answer
"It isn't enough to cut a sentence out of a war context and turn it into a rule for dealing with every non-Muslim. We have to ask who is being addressed, what the event was, and what limits surround the text. Draz reads fighting within the framework of treaties, aggression and peace."

### Sources
- Madkhal ila al-Quran al-Karim, PDF pp. 57–64 approx.

## BH-JH-003 - How can fighting be reconciled with "There is no compulsion in religion"?

### Question variants
- "No compulsion in religion" contradicts jihad.
- If Islam permits fighting, where is freedom of belief?
- Is fighting meant to force people into Islam?

### How the objection is framed
The objection assumes that the aim of every fight is to change the opponent's belief.

### Short answer
The contradiction disappears once we distinguish war as a political and military relationship from forcing the conscience to believe.

### Detailed answer
Faith in the heart is not produced by weapons, and the existence of rulings for coexisting with non-Muslims assumes that differences of belief continue even within an Islamic political order.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The sphere of belief is separate from the sphere of war.
- The existence of legislation for non-Muslims indicates that religious conversion was not a condition of every political relationship.

### Spoken answer
"Fighting and religious coercion are not synonyms. A war can happen for political or defensive reasons without a change in individuals' beliefs being a condition for ending it. That's why the sphere of war has to be separated from the sphere of belief."

### Sources
- Madkhal ila al-Quran al-Karim, PDF pp. 57–67.

## BH-JH-004 - Was booty the real motive for the conquests?

### Question variants
- Muslims fought for money.
- Religion was a cover for expansion and spoils.
- Is jihad an economic project?

### How the objection is framed
The objection reduces a complex historical and religious movement to a single material motive.

### Short answer
Draz rejects one-dimensional materialist reduction and connects the movement to a religious, moral and legal context, and to restrictions on fighting, which the pursuit of booty alone does not explain.

### Detailed answer
The presence of spoils in ancient wars does not prove that they caused the religion or were the only aim of war. Sacrifice, discipline, social construction and law also need explaining.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- A single-cause explanation does not account for all the data.
- A possible material benefit does not prove it was the only motive.

### Spoken answer
"The presence of spoils in an ancient war doesn't prove that spoils were the reason for the religion or its only goal. To prove that, we'd need an explanation of the law, the sacrifices, the moral restrictions and the structure of the society that emerged. Draz sees the purely economic explanation as narrower than the phenomenon."

### Sources
- Madkhal ila al-Quran al-Karim, PDF pp. 52–67.
