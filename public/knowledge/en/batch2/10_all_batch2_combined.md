# General Objections About Islam — Muhammad Qutb

## BH-GEN-001 - Has religion exhausted its purposes?

### Question variants
- Religion's function has ended
- Why do we need religion alongside science?

### How the objection is framed
The objection assumes religion was a historical stage that served its purpose before science, and that material progress makes it unnecessary.

### Short answer
The author responds that scientific progress does not remove the human need for purpose, value and a moral standard; science describes means, but cannot by itself determine the meaning of life or good and evil.

### Detailed answer
Qutb distinguishes the question of "how", which experimental science answers, from the question of "why", which concerns purpose, value and the standard of good and evil. Science describes means and increases capability, but does not by itself decide what that capability should be used for. The moral crises of materially advanced societies show that the need for a standard of values has not disappeared. For this reason he presents Islam as a comprehensive conception of life and purpose, not merely rituals that time can outgrow.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Separating empirical knowledge from values.
- Moral crises do not disappear automatically with material progress.
- For him, Islam is a conception of life, not merely rituals.

### Spoken answer
"Scientific progress is real, but it answers how things work, not why we live or what is good and evil. That is Qutb's point: the need for purpose and value remains, so science does not exhaust the function of religion."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Has Religion Exhausted Its Purposes?", p. 17 ff.

## BH-GEN-002 - Did Islam approve of slavery?

### Question variants
- Islam legislated slavery
- Why didn't it prohibit slavery immediately?

### How the objection is framed
The objection says Islam approved slavery and did not abolish it at once, and so was complicit in an unjust system.

### Short answer
The author presents slavery as a global institution that predated Islam, then focuses on how Islam narrowed its sources, widened the doors to emancipation and improved the condition of slaves. He sees the legislative direction as reducing the phenomenon, not establishing it.

### Detailed answer
Qutb begins by noting that slavery was a global institution that predated Islam, with many sources, so the question is not whether Islam created it but what Islam did with it. He then presents the direction of the legislation: narrowing the sources of enslavement, widening the doors of emancipation through manumission contracts and expiations, and raising the human and legal dignity of slaves. He sees this overall direction as indicating an intent to reduce the institution gradually, not to establish it. The distinction needed is between inheriting a historical reality and creating or promoting it.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Distinguishing between inheriting a historical reality and creating it.
- Expanding the means of liberation: manumission contracts (mukataba) and expiations (kaffarat).
- Raising the legal and moral dignity of slaves.

### Spoken answer
"Slavery was a global system before Islam. Qutb points out that Islam narrowed its sources, opened the doors to emancipation through manumission contracts and expiations, and raised the dignity of slaves, so the whole direction is toward reducing the practice, not founding it."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and Slavery", pp. 37–63

## BH-GEN-003 - Is Islam a feudal system?

### Question variants
- Islam entrenches feudalism
- Land ownership means feudalism

### How the objection is framed
The objection links land ownership in Islam to the European feudal system and concludes that Islam entrenches landlords' domination of peasants.

### Short answer
The author distinguishes between owning land and feudalism, which gives a lord authority over people and ties the peasant to the land. He holds that the structure of Islamic rights does not make feudalism a foundation of the system.

### Detailed answer
Feudalism in reality is a lord's authority over persons and the tying of the peasant to the land, not merely owning land. Qutb holds that the structure of rights in Islam separates owning land from owning the worker, and limits the owner's power through rights and duties. The owner is restricted in how he uses his property and bound by the rights of others in it. Injustice by landlords in history is not evidence that the legislative principle itself is feudal.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Owning the land does not mean owning the worker.
- Rights and duties limit the owner's power.
- Historical injustice is not the same as the legislative principle.

### Spoken answer
"Owning land is one thing and feudalism is another. Feudalism is authority over persons that ties the peasant to the land. Qutb sees that rights and duties in Islam limit the owner's power, so feudalism is not a foundation of the system."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and Feudalism", pp. 64–74

## BH-GEN-004 - Is Islam capitalist?

### Question variants
- Islam supports capitalism
- Permitting profit makes Islam capitalist

### How the objection is framed
The objection claims that permitting ownership and profit makes Islam a capitalist system, and that its ethics do not change its essence.

### Short answer
In the author's presentation, Islam affirms ownership and profit but restricts them through zakat, the prohibition of usury (riba), monopoly and fraud, and the rights of society; therefore it does not match absolute capitalism.

### Detailed answer
Qutb presents Islam as affirming private ownership and profit but restricting them with limits that are not part of absolute capitalism: zakat, the prohibition of usury, the prevention of monopoly and fraud, and respect for the rights of society. Ownership is a restricted right, not an absolute one, and the source of wealth and the way it is spent are governed by ethics. The rights of the poor and social solidarity are part of the structure of the system, not occasional charity. So similarity in recognizing ownership is not enough to equate it with capitalism.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Ownership is a restricted right, not an absolute one.
- The source of wealth and the way it is used are governed by ethics.
- The rights of the poor and social solidarity are part of the system.

### Spoken answer
"Islam accepts ownership and profit, but with limits: zakat, the ban on usury, monopoly and fraud, and rights owed to society. That is why Qutb sees it as not matching absolute capitalism; ownership is a restricted right, not an absolute one."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and Capitalism", pp. 75–84

## BH-GEN-005 - Does private ownership contradict justice?

### Question variants
- Private ownership is the cause of injustice
- Why doesn't Islam abolish private property?

### How the objection is framed
The objection says private ownership is the root of injustice and inequality, and that real justice requires abolishing it.

### Short answer
The author holds that justice does not require abolishing ownership, but regulating it through responsibility, the prevention of harm and financial obligations, while preserving the incentive to work and take initiative.

### Detailed answer
Qutb sees the source of injustice not in ownership itself but in its misuse. His remedy is to regulate it: the owner's responsibility, preventing harm to others, and the financial rights owed from wealth. This regulation preserves the incentive to work and take initiative, which is lost when ownership is abolished. Social rights restrict wealth, and individual incentive does not contradict solidarity.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The difference between ownership and its misuse.
- Social rights restrict wealth.
- Individual incentive does not contradict solidarity.

### Spoken answer
"For Qutb, justice does not require abolishing ownership but regulating it through responsibility, preventing harm and financial rights, while keeping the incentive to work. There is a big difference between ownership and its misuse."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and Private Ownership", pp. 85–94

## BH-GEN-006 - Does Islam endorse social classes?

### Question variants
- Islam is a class system
- The existence of rich and poor is classism

### How the objection is framed
The objection accuses Islam of endorsing a class system because a Muslim society contains rich and poor.

### Short answer
The author distinguishes between real economic disparity and closed legal classes with hereditary privilege, and affirms equality in dignity and in religious obligation.

### Detailed answer
Qutb distinguishes two things: real economic disparity, found in every society, and closed legal classes whose members inherit privilege. Islam does not recognize the second: there is no hereditary religious privilege, and people are equal in dignity and obligation. Wealth gives its owner no higher human worth, and solidarity limits the effects of material disparity.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- No hereditary religious privilege.
- Wealth does not confer higher human worth.
- Solidarity limits the effects of disparity.

### Spoken answer
"The existence of rich and poor does not mean a class system. Qutb separates real economic disparity from closed legal classes with hereditary privilege, and affirms equality in dignity and obligation."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and the Class System", pp. 95–100

## BH-GEN-007 - Is charity merely a patchwork solution to poverty?

### Question variants
- Islam tells the poor to wait for charity
- Zakat does not solve poverty

### How the objection is framed
The objection describes zakat and charity as a temporary sedative that asks the poor to wait for benevolence instead of treating the causes of poverty.

### Short answer
The author distinguishes between voluntary giving and the system of obligatory financial rights, work and solidarity, so he does not reduce the treatment of poverty to passing benevolence.

### Detailed answer
Qutb distinguishes voluntary giving from a system of obligatory financial rights of which solidarity and work are part. Zakat is an organized right, not a favor, and solidarity goes beyond individual charity. Preventing exploitation and requiring work are also part of treating poverty. So the system cannot be reduced to passing benevolence and then dismissed as a patch.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Zakat is an organized right.
- Solidarity goes beyond individual charity.
- Preventing exploitation and encouraging work are part of the remedy.

### Spoken answer
"Qutb separates voluntary giving from a system of obligatory financial rights. Zakat is an organized right, and solidarity, work and preventing exploitation are all part of the remedy, so it is not reducible to passing charity."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and Charity", pp. 101–105

## BH-GEN-009 - Are Islamic punishments brutal?

### Question variants
- The hudud (fixed penalties) are harsh
- Cutting off the hand is against mercy

### How the objection is framed
The objection holds that the hudud, such as cutting off the hand, are harsh punishments that conflict with mercy and human rights.

### Short answer
The author holds that a punishment cannot be assessed apart from the crime, the conditions of proof, prior prevention, deterrence and the protection of society.

### Detailed answer
Qutb holds that a punishment cannot be judged apart from its context: the crime, the conditions of proof, prior prevention, deterrence and the protection of society. The conditions of application are part of the ruling itself, and the safeguards prevent haste in punishing. Deterrence and protecting society are recognized objectives. So judging the punishment alone, without this system, is an incomplete judgment.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The conditions of application are part of the ruling.
- Deterrence and protecting society are recognized objectives.
- Safeguards prevent haste in punishing.

### Spoken answer
"Qutb sees that a punishment should not be assessed apart from the crime, the conditions of proof, prior prevention, deterrence and protecting society. The conditions of application and the safeguards are part of the ruling."

### Sources
- Shubuhat Hawl al-Islam — Muhammad Qutb, Chapter "Islam and Punishments", pp. 150–156

- Islam is unfit for the modern age

The objection says calling for the application of Sharia is a return to the past, and that Islam is unsuited to a different age.

### Short answer
The author rejects treating the age of an idea as proof that it is false, and distinguishes between the permanence of the principle and the changing of means and historical forms.

Qutb rejects treating the age of an idea as proof that it is false, since ideas are judged by their soundness, not their history. He distinguishes the permanence of the principle from the changing of means and historical forms; the constants are not historical molds. The renewal intended lies in applying the principles to reality, not in abolishing the foundations.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The age of an idea does not prove it wrong.
- Constants are not the same as historical molds.
- Renewal lies in application, not in abolishing the foundations.

Freedom in his view is tied to responsibility, so regulation is not psychological denial but organization.

# Objections About the Sunnah and Hadith — Muhammad Muhammad Abu Shahba

## BH-SUN-001 - Why do we need the Sunnah alongside the Quran?

### Question variants
- The Quran is enough for us
- What is the function of the Sunnah?

### How the objection is framed
The objection rests on the claim that the Quran alone is sufficient and the Sunnah is an unnecessary addition.

### Short answer
Abu Shahba explains that the Quran itself commands obedience to the Messenger and assigns him the function of explanation, and that the details of prayer, zakat, Hajj and other rites are known in practice through the Sunnah.

### Detailed answer
Abu Shahba explains that the Quran itself made obedience to the Messenger a principle and assigned him the function of explanation. The details of worship, such as the number of units of prayer, the amounts of zakat and the rites of Hajj, are not known from the Quran in detail, but are known in practice through the Sunnah. So the Sunnah is an explanation of the Quran, not a rival to it, and that some of its rulings stand independently does not mean it contradicts the Quran.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The Sunnah is an explanation of the Quran.
- Obeying the Messenger is a Quranic principle.
- That some rulings of the Sunnah stand independently does not mean they conflict with the Quran.

### Spoken answer
"The Quran itself commanded obedience to the Messenger and gave him the function of explanation. Abu Shahba points out that the details of prayer, zakat and Hajj are known only through the Sunnah, so it explains the Quran and does not replace it."

### Sources
- In Defense of the Sunnah and Refuting the Doubts of Orientalists and Contemporary Writers — Muhammad Muhammad Abu Shahba, "The Status of the Sunnah in Religion", pp. 11–17

## BH-SUN-002 - Is the hadith of presenting the Sunnah to the Quran a proof for rejecting hadith?

### Question variants
- If we do not find the hadith in the Quran, we reject it
- Presenting the Sunnah to the Quran

### How the objection is framed
Those who deny the Sunnah cite a report that orders presenting hadiths to the Quran and rejecting what does not agree with it, in order to nullify the Sunnah's authority.

### Short answer
The author examines the report and judges it unestablished, and holds that a weak report cannot be used to cancel the foundation of the Sunnah's authority, which is established by the Quran.

### Detailed answer
Abu Shahba examines the report itself and judges it unestablished, so a weak report cannot be used to cancel a foundation established by the Quran, the authority of the Sunnah. The sound method is to examine whether the report is authentic first, then to reconcile the texts. Not every prophetic ruling is required to appear verbatim in the Quran in order to be accepted.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Examine whether the report is authentic first.
- Reconciling the texts.
- Not every prophetic ruling is required to appear verbatim in the Quran.

### Spoken answer
"Abu Shahba examines the report on presenting the Sunnah to the Quran and finds it unestablished; a weak report cannot be used to cancel the Sunnah's authority, which the Quran itself established."

### Sources
- In Defense of the Sunnah and Refuting the Doubts of Orientalists and Contemporary Writers — Muhammad Muhammad Abu Shahba, "The Hadith of Presenting the Sunnah to the Quran Is Fabricated", p. 18

- Why wasn't the Sunnah written down?

The objection says the Prophet forbade writing hadith, which shows the Sunnah was not documented in his time.

### Short answer
The author reconciles the reports of prohibition and permission, and explains the early prohibition as a fear that hadith would be mixed with the Quran, while writing is established in the Prophetic era.

### Detailed answer
Abu Shahba reconciles the reports of prohibition with the reports of permission to write. He explains the early prohibition as a fear that hadith would be mixed with the Quran before the two were distinguished, while writing is established in the Prophetic era and early hadith sheets existed. The prohibition was not permanent, and context explains the difference between the reports.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The prohibition was not permanent.
- Early hadith sheets (suhuf) existed.
- Context explains the difference between the reports.

### Spoken answer
"Abu Shahba reconciles the reports of prohibition and permission: the early ban was out of fear of mixing hadith with the Quran, and writing is established in the Prophetic era, so the ban was not permanent."

## BH-SUN-004 - Was hadith not compiled until two centuries later?

### Question variants
- Al-Bukhari came 200 years later
- Late compilation weakens the Sunnah

### How the objection is framed
The objection claims that al-Bukhari and others compiled hadith about two centuries later, so it cannot be trusted.

### Short answer
The author distinguishes between early writing, general compilation (tadwin) and encyclopedic classification; the lateness of some major compilations does not mean the material was absent before them.

### Detailed answer
Abu Shahba distinguishes three stages: early individual writing, general compilation which was a stage of organization, and encyclopedic classification. The lateness of some major compilations does not mean the material was absent before them, since transmission was both oral and written. So the date of a book's classification is not the date the hadiths appeared.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Early individual writing.
- General compilation was a stage of organization.
- Transmission was both oral and written.

### Spoken answer
"Abu Shahba separates early writing, general compilation and encyclopedic classification; the lateness of some major works does not mean the material did not exist and circulate before them."

### Sources
- In Defense of the Sunnah and Refuting the Doubts of Orientalists and Contemporary Writers — Muhammad Muhammad Abu Shahba, "Writing Hadiths", "Compiling Them" and "Stages of Compilation", pp. 22–29

## BH-SUN-005 - Did hadith scholars care about the chain and neglect the text?

### Question variants
- Hadith criticism is formalistic
- Any text is accepted with a sound chain

### How the objection is framed
The objection describes the hadith scholars' criticism as formalistic, stopping at the chain and accepting any text as long as its chain is sound.

### Short answer
The author presents the criticism of both chains and texts together, and lists hidden defects ('ilal), irregularity (shudhudh), objectionability (nakara), inconsistency (idtirab) and contradiction among the tools of criticism.

### Detailed answer
Abu Shahba shows that the hadith scholars criticized both chains and texts, and made hidden defects, irregularity, objectionability, inconsistency and contradiction among their tools of criticism. Both chain and text are examined, and defects go beyond the names of narrators to the content of the report. Caution in criticism does not mean its absence.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Both chain and text are examined.
- Defects go beyond the names of narrators.
- Caution in criticism does not mean its absence.

### Spoken answer
"Abu Shahba shows that the hadith scholars criticized both chains and texts, using tools such as hidden defects, irregularity, objectionability, inconsistency and contradiction, so the criticism was not merely formal."

### Sources
- In Defense of the Sunnah and Refuting the Doubts of Orientalists and Contemporary Writers — Muhammad Muhammad Abu Shahba, "The Hadith Scholars' Care for Criticism and Understanding", pp. 28–35

## BH-SUN-006 - How did the hadith scholars authenticate narrators?

### Question variants
- Is it enough for a narrator to be righteous?
- A chain can easily be fabricated

### How the objection is framed
The objection doubts that a narrator's righteousness is enough to accept his report, and holds that chains can easily be fabricated.

### Short answer
The author presents the conditions of probity ('adala), precision (dabt) and continuity of the chain, as well as comparing reports and tracing errors, so personal righteousness alone is not sufficient.

### Detailed answer
Abu Shahba shows that personal righteousness alone is not enough; probity, precision and continuity of the chain are required, along with comparing reports and tracing errors. Probity and precision are two independent conditions, comparing routes exposes error, and the science of narrators and of defects is part of the method.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Probity and precision are two independent conditions.
- Comparing routes exposes error.
- The science of narrators and of defects is part of the method.

### Spoken answer
"Abu Shahba presents the conditions of probity, precision and continuity, plus comparing reports and tracking errors, so a narrator's personal righteousness alone is not enough."

### Sources
- In Defense of the Sunnah and Refuting the Doubts of Orientalists and Contemporary Writers — Muhammad Muhammad Abu Shahba, "Conditions of Acceptable Narration", pp. 30–33

## BH-SUN-007 - Did narration by meaning corrupt the Sunnah?

### Question variants
- The hadiths are not the Prophet's wording
- The narrators changed the texts

### How the objection is framed
The objection claims that what we have is not the Prophet's wording but the narrators' phrasing, and that they changed the texts.

### Short answer
