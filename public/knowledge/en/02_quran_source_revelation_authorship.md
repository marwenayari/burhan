# The Source of the Quran and Revelation

## BH-QS-001 - Did Muhammad ﷺ write the Quran himself?

### Question variants
- The Quran was authored by Muhammad.
- Perhaps Muhammad was a literary genius who wrote the Quran.
- Muhammad authored the Quran.

### How the objection is framed
The objection assumes that personal authorship explains the characteristics and history of the Quran.

### Short answer
Draz builds a cumulative answer from the difference between the style of the Quran and the Prophet's own speech, the verses of reproach and correction, the nature of revelation, and content that the hypothesis of a personal author does not easily explain.

### Detailed answer
The argument is not a single point. It is the combination of the Quran's independent style, texts that correct the Prophet's own judgments, his waiting for revelation, and the nature of the information and details the Quran discusses.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The stylistic difference between the hadith and the Quran.
- Verses that reproach or correct the Prophet.
- The Prophet appears as a recipient of revelation, not in control of when it came.

### Spoken answer
"The hypothesis that Muhammad wrote the Quran has to explain why the Quran's style differs from his known speech, why the text contains reproach of him and corrections of some of his judgments, and why he consistently appears as a recipient waiting for revelation. Draz brings these indications together and does not rely on just one."

### Sources
- Al-Naba' al-'Azim (The Great News), sections on the source of the Quran, PDF pp. 55–110 approx.
- Madkhal ila al-Quran al-Karim (Introduction to the Noble Quran), section on the divine source of the Quran.

## BH-QS-002 - Did the Prophet learn the Quran from Jews or Christians?

### Question variants
- Muhammad took the stories from the People of the Book.
- Perhaps he had a secret Jewish or Christian teacher.
- The similarity with the Bible proves borrowing.

### How the objection is framed
The objection infers a direct human teacher from shared subject matter.

### Short answer
Draz distinguishes between a shared subject and proof of a path of borrowing, and points out that the Quran often debates the People of the Book, criticizes them and corrects their views rather than appearing to follow them.

### Detailed answer
General knowledge of some prophets' names does not explain the precise details. And the teacher hypothesis needs to identify the teacher, the path of transmission and the historical evidence, not just point to similarity.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Similarity alone does not prove a relationship of transmission.
- The Quran criticizes the People of the Book in many places.
- Historical evidence of the teacher and the means of learning is required.

### Spoken answer
"The story of Moses or Mary appearing in both the Quran and the Bible isn't enough to prove borrowing. We need evidence of who taught him and how. And Draz notes that the Quran often disagrees with the People of the Book and criticizes them, so the similarity proves a shared subject, not a path of transmission."

### Sources
- Al-Naba' al-'Azim, PDF pp. 55–60, and p. 90 in particular.

## BH-QS-003 - Were the stories of the prophets just popular tales?

### Question variants
- The stories of the prophets were known among the Arabs.
- The Quran reworded folk tales.
- There is nothing strange about Muhammad knowing stories of the past.

### How the objection is framed
The objection treats general knowledge of names and events as equivalent to detailed, organized knowledge.

### Short answer
Draz accepts that a small amount of such reports may have been widespread, but distinguishes this from the precise details and critical positions that popular knowledge alone does not explain.

### Detailed answer
Names such as Noah, Moses and Mary being present in the environment is not the same as possessing detailed, organized material about them.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- The source states openly that some general reports may have circulated.
- The core of the argument is the difference between the general outline and the details.

### Spoken answer
"Draz doesn't say the Arabs had never heard the name of Noah or Moses. He distinguishes between general knowledge passed on in tales and precise details and coherent critical positions. A name being widespread doesn't explain all of the Quranic material."

### Sources
- Al-Naba' al-'Azim, PDF pp. 55–60 approx.

## BH-QS-004 - Was revelation just a psychological state or an inner voice?

### Question variants
- Perhaps revelation was a psychological state.
- Was Gabriel just an inner image?
- How do we know it wasn't hallucination?

### How the objection is framed
The objection tries to explain the prophetic experience as something inside the mind, with no external source.

### Short answer
Draz presents revelation as an experience with a consistent pattern and coherent content, while the Prophet's mental balance remained intact, and distinguishes it from psychological disorders or ordinary human suggestion.

### Detailed answer
The Prophet distinguishes between his personal opinion and revelation; the experience does not leave him irrational or with a disturbed personality, and the content remains coherent over many years.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- His mental balance continued.
- He distinguished between his opinion and revelation.
- The content stayed coherent over a long period.

### Spoken answer
"Explaining revelation as a psychological state isn't an automatic conclusion; it's a hypothesis that needs evidence. Draz asks whether the signs of the experience, its content and the Prophet's continued balance resemble a psychological disorder, and he notes that the Prophet distinguished between his human opinion and revelation."

### Sources
- Al-Naba' al-'Azim, study of the phenomenon of revelation, PDF pp. 100–115 approx., including p. 110.

## BH-QS-005 - Why is the Quran's reproach of the Prophet an indication against personal authorship?

### Question variants
- Perhaps Muhammad wrote the verses of reproach to appear sincere.
- Why do the verses of reproach matter?
- Does the Quran criticize the Prophet?

### How the objection is framed
The objection argues that an author can deliberately write criticism of himself.

### Short answer
The verses of reproach are not a proof on their own, but they are one indication among several: they sometimes show the text correcting the Prophet in situations that a personal author could have skipped or worded in his own favor.

### Detailed answer
The book gives examples of the Quran being revealed to correct a ruling or position of the Prophet. Their value becomes clear when combined with the other indications that he was a recipient of the text.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- It is not a standalone argument.
- It gains its strength from context, repetition and its combination with the other indications.

### Spoken answer
"The verses of reproach don't settle the debate on their own, but they stand out within the full picture. If someone were writing a text to strengthen his authority, texts that publicly correct some of his positions need explaining. Under the hypothesis of revelation this is natural, because he is a recipient of the text."

### Sources
- Al-Naba' al-'Azim, examples of reproach and correction, PDF pp. 75–85 approx., including the story of Banu Ubayriq.

## BH-QS-006 - Were the Quran's prophecies written after the events happened?

### Question variants
- Perhaps the prophecies were added after the events took place.
- Predicting the future proves nothing if the text was edited later.
- How do we verify when the text was written?

### How the objection is framed
The objection points to the need to prove that the text came before the event.

### Short answer
Draz's method implicitly agrees that the value of a statement about the future depends on when the text was in circulation, and he presents examples of promises of victory and security made in early contexts of weakness and fear.

### Detailed answer
The right question is not only whether a prophecy exists, but whether the text was known and in circulation before the event happened. That is why the author connects the verses to their historical context.

### Evidence and line of reasoning
- Documenting the timing of the text is an important condition.
- The author places the verses in contexts of weakness that came before the promise was fulfilled.

### Spoken answer
"A prophecy isn't evidence if we don't know when the text appeared. So the real question is: was the verse in circulation before the event happened? Draz presents some of the promises in their context, when the Muslims were afraid and weak, and then compares them with what happened later."

### Sources
- Al-Naba' al-'Azim, examples of statements about the future, PDF p. 65 and following.
